Spend enough time looking at Seikos and eventually somebody will tell you they just bought a Turtle.

Then somebody else will mention their Tuna.

Before long you're looking at a Willard, an Arnie and something called a Baby MarineMaster and wondering why nobody is using the numbers printed on the watches.

Welcome to Seiko collecting.

Most of these nicknames weren't invented by Seiko. Collectors came up with them because saying "Turtle" is considerably easier than remembering 6309-7040. Some became so common that Seiko eventually started using them too. Each entry below is marked **Seiko name**, **Collector name**, or **Collector name, adopted by Seiko**.

Here's what everyone is talking about.

## 62MAS {seiko}

**The one that started it.**

The 62MAS was Seiko's first dive watch and Japan's first, introduced in 1965. The original references were 6217-8000 and 6217-8001, with an automatic movement and 150 meters of water resistance.

Unlike most of the names on this page, 62MAS isn't a nickname somebody made up because the watch looked like an animal. It comes from Seiko's own catalog shorthand for the caliber 62 Seikomatic Selfdater family, and Seiko now routinely calls the watch the 62MAS.

More importantly, it established a case and dial design Seiko has returned to again and again. Modern watches like the SLA017, SJE093, SPB143 and my SBDX041 descend from it. If somebody says a modern Seiko is "62MAS-inspired," this is what they mean.

**Original:** 6217-8000 / 6217-8001
**Why the name:** Seiko's catalog name for the caliber 62 Seikomatic Selfdater

## Willard {collector}

**The Apocalypse Now Seiko.**

The 6105-8110/8119 arrived in 1970 with the big asymmetrical cushion case and crown at four o'clock that became part of the Seiko diver look.

Its nickname came later. Martin Sheen wore a 6105 as Captain Benjamin Willard in *Apocalypse Now*, so collectors started calling it the Captain Willard, or usually just the Willard.

Seiko has since made modern interpretations, including the SPB151 and its relatives. In Japan, the same watch is also associated with explorer Naomi Uemura, who wore a 6105 on his expeditions.

**Original:** 6105-8110 / 6105-8119
**Why the name:** Captain Willard in *Apocalypse Now*

## Turtle {adopted}

**The one that actually looks like its nickname.**

The classic Turtle arrived in the 1970s with the 6306 and 6309 divers. Seen from above, the rounded cushion case looks like a turtle shell. That's pretty much the entire story.

The best-known vintage references are the 6306-7000/7001 and the export-market 6309-7040/7049. Seiko revived the shape in 2016 with watches like the SRP777, and the Turtle has been with us ever since. There are now King Turtles, Save the Ocean Turtles and enough other versions to form a small reptile exhibit.

**Classic references:** 6306-7000/7001, 6309-7040/7049
**Modern example:** SRP777
**Why the name:** The turtle-shell-shaped case

## Mini Turtle {collector}

Take a Turtle, shrink the idea, move the crown to three o'clock and you get the Mini Turtle.

The modern series appeared around 2017 with references including the SRPC35, SRPC37, SRPC39 and SRPC41. It's not literally a scaled-down Turtle, but the family resemblance was enough for collectors.

**Examples:** SRPC35 / SRPC37
**Why the name:** A smaller, Turtle-like case

## Tuna {adopted}

**A watch named after canned fish. Somehow it works.**

In 1968, Seiko received a letter from a professional saturation diver saying existing dive watches weren't good enough for the conditions he worked in. Seiko spent seven years on the problem.

The result was the 1975 Professional Diver's 600m, reference 6159-7010: a titanium monocoque case, a protective outer shroud, an L-shaped gasket, an accordion strap and a design unlike almost anything else.

That round shroud reminded collectors of a tuna can. The name stuck. "Tuna" now describes a whole branch of large, shrouded Seiko professional divers rather than one reference.

**Original:** 6159-7010 Professional Diver's 600m
**Why the name:** It looks like a tuna can

## Grandfather Tuna {collector}

The original 1975 6159-7010 is often called the Grandfather Tuna because it's the watch that started the whole Tuna family. Not particularly complicated.

**Reference:** 6159-7010
**Why the name:** The original ancestor of the Tuna line

## Darth Tuna {collector}

An all-black Tuna. Collectors looked at a giant black dive watch and decided Darth Vader was the obvious next step.

The name is best known on the blacked-out SBBN011, and it carried over to later black versions like the SBBN013.

**Common example:** SBBN011
**Why the name:** Large, black and vaguely Imperial

## Emperor Tuna {collector}

"Emperor Tuna" is generally used for the top-end mechanical 1000-meter Tunas, such as the SBDX011. It's collector shorthand for a very serious Tuna.

**Common example:** SBDX011
**Why the name:** A big mechanical Tuna near the top of the food chain

## Arnie {collector}

**Arnold Schwarzenegger's Seiko.**

The H558 was an early analog-digital dive watch from the 1980s, with a shrouded case, analog hands and a digital display. Schwarzenegger wore it on screen during his 1980s action-movie run, including *Commando* and *Predator*. Collectors did the rest.

Seiko revived the idea with solar models such as the SNJ025.

**Original family:** H558
**Modern example:** SNJ025
**Why the name:** Arnold Schwarzenegger

## Monster {adopted}

The first-generation Monster appeared around 2000, most famously as the black SKX779 and orange SKX781.

The huge luminous markers, aggressive hands and deeply scalloped bezel made it look like something with teeth. Seiko's own designers have said the bezel and minute markers can look like a monster opening its mouth.

There have been several generations since, plus Black Monsters, Orange Monsters and assorted limited versions.

**Original examples:** SKX779 / SKX781
**Why the name:** The aggressive bezel, markers and generally monstrous look

## Samurai {adopted}

The Samurai appeared in the mid-2000s and is one of the easiest Seiko divers to recognize, thanks to its angular case.

There are several stories about where the nickname came from. The sword-like hands get mentioned. So do the sharply cut case surfaces and their resemblance to Japanese armor. Even Seiko's own discussion of the name treats it as interpretation rather than documented fact.

Which is another way of saying nobody really knows who first called it a Samurai. But everybody does now.

**Early family:** SBDA series
**Modern example:** SRPB51
**Why the name:** Probably the angular, blade-like Japanese design cues

## Sumo {adopted}

The Sumo debuted in 2007, with the SBDC001 as the classic black example. It's a large watch with a muscular case, so "Sumo" isn't much of a stretch.

Seiko has offered two explanations: the stout, powerful shape looks like a sumo wrestler, and the large marker at 12 o'clock looks like a wrestler's topknot. Nobody seems to know which came first.

**Original example:** SBDC001
**Why the name:** The big, muscular case, possibly helped by the 12 o'clock marker

## Shogun {adopted}

The Shogun is another big Prospex diver, but with a much more angular case than the Sumo, and it has traditionally been made of titanium. Early examples include the SBDC007 and SBDC009.

Seiko says the sharp indexes, bezel and metal surfaces reminded fans of a shogun's armor.

**Early examples:** SBDC007 / SBDC009
**Why the name:** An angular, metallic design that recalls Japanese armor

## MarineMaster / MM300 {seiko}

Here's where things get confusing. **MarineMaster is a real Seiko name.**

The best-known modern example is the SBDX001, a serious 300-meter mechanical diver with an 8L35 movement and a one-piece case. Its successor, the SBDX017, is the one I own. Collectors shorten MarineMaster 300 to **MM300**.

Seiko has changed how it uses the MarineMaster name over the years, so don't assume every watch somebody calls a "MarineMaster" belongs to exactly the same family.

**Classic example:** SBDX001
**Why the name:** A Seiko product name; MM300 is collector shorthand

## Baby MarineMaster / MM200 {collector}

Now we're back in nickname territory.

In 2018 Seiko released a family of 200-meter divers including the SBDC061/SPB077. They borrowed enough from Seiko's higher-end professional divers that collectors started calling them the **Baby MarineMaster**, or **MarineMaster 200**, or **MM200**.

Seiko didn't put Baby MarineMaster on the dial. Collectors did that. The name is also a little messy, because people don't always agree on which later references belong under the MM200 umbrella.

**Early example:** SBDC061 / SPB077
**Why the name:** It looks like the more expensive MarineMaster, at 200 meters

## Great Blue Hole {collector}

One from my own collection.

The SPB083J1, sold in Japan as the SBDC065, is the blue member of the early MM200, or Baby MarineMaster, family. It's commonly known as the **Great Blue Hole**, after its dramatic blue gradient dial.

Mine has so far explored the terrifying depths of my pool.

**Reference:** SPB083J1 / SBDC065
**Family:** MM200 / Baby MarineMaster
**Why the name:** The blue gradient dial and the Great Blue Hole off Belize

## Pogue {collector}

One of the better Seiko stories.

NASA astronaut Colonel William Pogue bought a Seiko 6139 automatic chronograph and used it while training for the Skylab 4 mission. Then he took it to space.

The yellow-dial 6139-600x associated with Pogue became known simply as the Pogue. It wasn't NASA-issued; his official mission watch was an Omega Speedmaster. The Seiko was his own, which arguably makes the story better.

Be careful with listings: sellers sometimes call almost any yellow 6139 a Pogue, and reference details vary by market.

**Family:** Yellow-dial 6139-600x
**Why the name:** Astronaut William Pogue

## Bullhead {collector}

Look at it from the top and this one makes sense immediately. On the 6138-004x chronographs, the crown and pushers sit at the top of the case rather than the side. They look like horns. Bullhead.

**References:** 6138-004x
**Why the name:** Top-mounted pushers that look like a bull's horns

## UFO / Yachtsman {collector}

The 6138-001x is a big, rounded, nearly lugless 1970s chronograph. Collectors thought it looked like a flying saucer, hence UFO.

You'll also see Yachtsman, a name from period American marketing. Same watch, two names, because apparently one wasn't enough.

**References:** 6138-001x
**Why UFO:** The saucer-shaped case
**Why Yachtsman:** A period distributor and marketing name

## Kakume {collector}

Another 6138 chronograph. The 6138-003x has two distinctive squarish registers, and "kakume" is Japanese for **square eyes**. Once you've seen the dial, you can't argue with it.

**References:** 6138-003x
**Why the name:** The square chronograph registers

## Panda {collector}

Not uniquely a Seiko nickname at all. A Panda chronograph has a light dial with dark subdials, like a panda's face.

Seiko's famous version is the 6138-8020, and Seiko itself has used "Panda" when talking about it.

**Classic Seiko Panda:** 6138-8020
**Why the name:** A white or silver dial with contrasting black subdials

## Ripley {collector}

One of Seiko's strangest watches, which is saying something.

The asymmetrical 7A28-7000 quartz chronograph was designed by Giorgetto Giugiaro and worn by Sigourney Weaver as Ellen Ripley in *Aliens*. Collectors call it the Ripley. Seiko later revisited the design with limited-edition reissues.

**Original:** 7A28-7000
**Why the name:** Ellen Ripley in *Aliens*

## Bishop {collector}

Ripley wasn't the only one in *Aliens* wearing a Giugiaro Seiko. The android Bishop, played by Lance Henriksen, wore the related 7A28-6000. So naturally that one became the Bishop.

**Original:** 7A28-6000
**Why the name:** Bishop in *Aliens*

## Sea Urchin {collector}

The Sea Urchin usually means Seiko 5 Sports models such as the SNZF17. It's not a true ISO dive watch, despite looking very much like one. The name is generally put down to the many projecting markers and bezel details, which look vaguely spiny.

**Common example:** SNZF17
**Why the name:** A spiny-looking dial and bezel

## Atlas / Landshark {collector}

Two nicknames for the same Seiko 5 Sports family, best known as the SKZ211.

It has an inner rotating compass scale controlled by a second crown, which helps explain Atlas. Landshark plays on its mix of land-adventure styling and 200 meters of water resistance.

**Common example:** SKZ211
**Why the names:** The compass theme, and its land-and-sea ability

## Spork {collector}

Yes, Spork.

The SRP043 combines a dive-watch case with a very legible, pilot-style dial. That led to the spoon-plus-fork explanation: part diver, part pilot.

There's another explanation that's at least as convincing: SRP043K looks suspiciously like SPORK if you've spent too long staring at Seiko reference numbers. Either way, Spork won.

**Reference:** SRP043
**Why the name:** A diver-pilot mashup, or a joke based on the reference number

## Stargate {collector}

The Stargate family gets its name from its unusual bezel, which collectors thought looked like the circular portal from *Stargate*. It makes more sense once you see the watch and the movie prop side by side.

**Example:** SKZ323
**Why the name:** The bezel looks like the *Stargate* portal

## Starfish {collector}

Another one you can identify without much research. The SKZ28x family has a bezel with large projecting sections that look like the arms of a starfish. Some collectors call it the Shuriken.

**Example:** SKZ283
**Why the name:** The pointed, star-shaped bezel

## Bottle Cap {collector}

The Seiko 5 Sports Bottle Cap has a tall, heavily notched round bezel. From the side, it looks like the crimped edge of a bottle cap. Sometimes Seiko collectors really do just call things what they look like.

**Example:** SRPC61
**Why the name:** The bottle-cap-shaped bezel

## Alpinist {seiko}

This one gets lumped in with the nicknames, but **Alpinist is a real Seiko name.**

The original Laurel Alpinist dates to 1959 and was made with mountain use in mind. Today people most often picture the green SARB017 and its compass-ring descendants, like my 2021 European edition, the SPB201. So if somebody calls a SARB017 an Alpinist, that's not an internet nickname. Seiko beat them to it.

**Famous modern example:** SARB017
**Why the name:** Seiko named the line for mountaineering

# And then it gets weird.

Dig into vintage Seiko collecting and you'll run into plenty more: **Jumbo** for some of the big 6138 chronographs, **Baby Panda** and **Baby Kakume** for smaller relatives of those two, **Calculator** for chronographs with slide-rule scales, and **Shuriken** for some Starfish-style watches. There are many more names for particular colors, regional editions and vintage references.

At some point this stops being a guide and becomes a cry for help.

# A word about Seiko nicknames

A nickname isn't a reference number.

"Tuna" describes a family. "Panda" describes a dial layout used by many brands. "Willard" points back to one historic design but is also used for its modern descendants. "Baby MarineMaster" was never an official Seiko name at all. And sellers aren't always consistent about any of it.

If you're buying an older Seiko, especially an expensive one, ignore the cute name for a minute and check the actual caliber, case number and reference. That's the part Seiko put on the watch.

The rest of us came up with the zoo.
